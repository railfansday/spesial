Martha Stewart's ventures, from Entertaining to Martha Stewart Living Radio

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 Martha Stewart business ventures, from a Connecticut catering company to a Macy's home line, trace how she turned entertaining know-how into a media and retail brand

Martha Stewart built a business in which her own name served as the product, the label and the editorial voice at once. Over the decades, that name has appeared on cookbooks, magazine covers, television programs, store shelves and a satellite radio channel.

Her path did not begin in media. Stewart worked as a stockbroker before she turned to food. In the 1970s, she started a catering business from her home in Westport, Connecticut. That business became the base for the entertaining advice she would later package for a national audience.

The next ventures moved that home expertise into books, retail and media. Her catering experience shaped her first book, Entertaining, which Clarkson Potter published in 1982. In 1987, she began consulting for Kmart. A magazine, Martha Stewart Living, launched in the early 1990s with Time Publishing Ventures, a Time Warner unit. A syndicated television show of the same name began in 1993.

The ventures share a common structure. Stewart took one subject, such as cooking, gardening, crafting or decorating, and offered it through several channels. A reader could find a project in the magazine, watch it on TV and then look for related products in a store or catalog. The approach turned household know-how into publishing, broadcasting and retail businesses.

Ownership became part of the story as well. Stewart bought back her brand rights from Time Warner and formed Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia in 1997. The company went public on the New York Stock Exchange in October 1999 under the ticker MSO. Sequential Brands acquired it in 2015.

This list covers a selection of 10 Martha Stewart business ventures, arranged roughly in the order they began. It is not a complete account of everything she has launched, and it is not a ranking. Each entry explains what the venture was, when it started and how it connected to the wider Martha Stewart brand. The list runs from a Connecticut catering kitchen to the Martha Stewart Collection at Macy's $M.

Martha Stewart started a catering business from her home in Westport, Connecticut, in the 1970s. That business became the base for the entertaining expertise later attached to her name.

Stewart came to catering from finance. She worked as a stockbroker before she started the business. She then left that career to cook for paying clients.

The two jobs had little in common. A stockbroker buys and sells securities on behalf of clients. A caterer plans and prepares food for events that clients host.

Catering differs from running a restaurant. A restaurant serves guests at its own premises. A caterer brings food to the client's event, whether that is a dinner party, a cocktail reception or a wedding.

Each job is planned around a single occasion. The caterer settles a menu with the client, cooks in advance and delivers the food ready to serve. Many caterers also supply serving staff and handle cleanup after guests leave. The work combines cooking with logistics, such as timing courses and arranging food for presentation.

Westport sits in Fairfield County, in the southwestern corner of Connecticut, on Long Island Sound. Stewart ran the operation from her own house there rather than from a separate commercial site.

The catering company is where her interest in food for gatherings became a business. Cooking for other people's parties became the subject that later ventures built on.

The link shows up directly in her publishing career. Her first book drew on the catering work. It moved her from serving clients one event at a time to selling her approach to a wider audience.

The later ventures carried the same theme into other formats. Magazines, television programs and home goods lines all returned to entertaining at home. Each of those formats traced its subject back to the Westport catering business.

Entertaining, Martha Stewart's first book, was published in 1982 by Clarkson Potter. It drew on her catering work and began her publishing career.

The book turned a service into a product. A catering job feeds the guests at one event. A book about hosting can reach any reader who buys a copy, wherever that reader lives.

Catering had given Stewart working knowledge of planning food for gatherings. Entertaining put that knowledge into print under her name. Its one-word title stated the subject plainly.

The move also changed how her expertise could earn money. A caterer is paid job by job. A book can keep selling after it is written, with no new event to staff or cook for.

A book also reaches people its author never meets. A caterer's clients deal directly with the person running the kitchen. A reader deals only with the advice on the page and the name on the cover.

Publishing put a partner between Stewart and her audience. A publisher such as Clarkson Potter produces and distributes the books it releases. The author supplies the content.

Entertaining is the earliest entry in her publishing record, and more books under her name followed it. It was the first time she offered her approach to hosting through print rather than through paid client work.

Martha Stewart began working with Kmart as a consultant in 1987. A decade later, in 1997, the retailer launched Martha Stewart Everyday, a home line sold under her name.

The two dates mark separate stages of one relationship. The first was an advisory role. The second put her name directly on merchandise in Kmart stores. Each stage asked something different of her name.

The difference matters for how a personal brand works. A consultant shapes decisions out of public view, and customers may never know who advised the store. A named product line ties a public figure's reputation to each item on the shelf. If a product disappoints a shopper, the name on the label absorbs some of that reaction.

With Everyday, shoppers could buy goods carrying the Martha Stewart name without picking up a book or a magazine. That gave the brand a physical presence with shoppers who might not read her magazine or watch her show. It also turned her advice into something a customer could hold, use and replace.

Kmart was a discount chain. The Everyday line placed her name in front of a mass-market retail audience shopping for ordinary household goods. Her media work had built a reputation around careful, detailed homemaking. The Kmart line carried that reputation into lower-priced goods sold to a broad customer base.

The decade between the consulting deal and the Everyday launch shows a retail business built in steps. Stewart spent years advising the chain before her name went on its products. The Kmart work ran alongside her publishing and television projects rather than replacing them.

The Kmart relationship eventually ended. Her retail partnerships did not end with it. The Martha Stewart Collection at Macy's $M, launched in 2007, later filled the role the Kmart-era arrangement had played. That move shifted the name from a discount chain to a department store.

Martha Stewart Living magazine launched in the early 1990s through Time Publishing Ventures, a unit of Time Warner. The title gave Stewart a regular publication of her own, backed by a large media company.

Before the magazine, her audience reached her through catering clients and books. A periodical works differently. A book reaches a reader once. A magazine comes back with each new issue. That lets a publisher keep returning to the same readers over time.

The recurring format suited the subjects she was already known for. Cooking, gardening, crafting and decorating all shift with the seasons. A periodical can follow those cycles from one issue to the next. A single book is fixed once it goes to print.

The partnership paired a personal brand with a corporate publisher. Time Publishing Ventures operated inside Time Warner, which put the new title within an established publishing business. A large publisher already has the printing, distribution and newsstand reach that a new title would otherwise have to assemble on its own.

The magazine put Stewart's own name at the front of its title. That tied the publication directly to her public identity rather than to a standalone masthead. It also made Martha Stewart Living a label that could extend beyond print.

Consumer magazines typically earn revenue from two groups at once. Readers pay for subscriptions and single copies. Advertisers pay to reach those readers. Under that model, the audience Stewart drew became something a publisher could sell to brands.

Martha Stewart moved her brand onto television in 1993 with a syndicated show called Martha Stewart Living. A second program, the daytime show The Martha Stewart Show, followed in 2005 and stayed on air into the early 2010s.

Syndication shaped how the first show reached viewers. A syndicated program is sold to individual stations rather than produced for one national network's schedule. Each station decides whether to carry the show and where to place it in its lineup. That model let a series appear in markets across the U.S. without a single network's commitment. It also meant airtimes could differ from one city to the next. Stations that carried it could also drop it, so the show had to hold its audience market by market.

Television suited the kind of material Stewart was known for. Cooking, crafts and home projects are hard to learn from text alone. A camera can show the order of steps, the texture of a dough or the way a fold is made. On the page, the same details need photographs and careful written instructions. On screen, a viewer can watch each technique being done.

Demonstration suits a how-to subject. A single segment can take one project, such as a recipe or a craft, and walk through it from start to finish. That mirrors the step-by-step structure of a recipe, where the result depends on doing things in order.

The Martha Stewart Show arrived 12 years after the first series. Daytime programs typically air on weekdays, so a daytime show reaches its audience several times a week rather than once. That pace calls for a steady supply of new segments.

For the business, the shows worked alongside print rather than replacing it. They put her name in front of viewers who might never pick up a magazine. A television slot also returns on a fixed schedule. That gives a brand regular contact with its audience between magazine issues.

Martha by Mail was a catalog of home and craft products that Martha Stewart launched in 1995. It sold goods straight to customers. That made it an early direct-to-consumer business under her name.

In a typical catalog business, a shopper browses printed pages at home, places an order and waits for delivery. The seller handles that customer relationship itself instead of passing it through a retailer. The name Martha by Mail spelled out that model in three words.

The arrangement also shifts work onto the seller. A catalog company has to source and stock its own merchandise, print and mail its pages, take orders and ship each package. In return, it keeps control over the product range, the presentation and the prices.

Direct selling also produces a customer list. Every order records a buyer's name and address. The seller can use that list to mail the next catalog without relying on a retailer to bring shoppers in.

That model set Martha by Mail apart from Stewart's work with Kmart. There, a national discount chain controlled the stores and the shelves. With the catalog, her own operation decided what to offer and how to show it.

The catalog had a ready audience in people who already followed Stewart's home and craft projects in print and on television. It gave them a way to buy goods from a business carrying her name.

Home and craft goods suit the mail-order format. Craft projects often call for specific materials and tools. A catalog can gather those items in one place. A shopper then does not have to search several stores to find them.

Martha by Mail arrived two years before Stewart formed Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia. It also predated widespread online shopping. Many later direct-to-consumer brands sell mainly through websites. The basic idea of a brand selling straight to its own audience was already at work in this catalog.

Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia went public on the New York Stock Exchange in October 1999, trading under the ticker MSO. The listing turned a company built around one person's name and know-how into a publicly traded business.

The company was only two years old at the time. In 1997, Stewart bought back the rights to her brand from Time Warner, whose publishing unit had backed the launch of Martha Stewart Living magazine. She then formed Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia to own and run that brand directly.

The buyback shifted control of the name. Under the earlier arrangement, a large media corporation held the rights to a brand that carried her name. Owning those rights gave Stewart's own company the say over how the Martha Stewart name was used.

The word Omnimedia described the company's intended scope. It was set up to house her work across several formats, including magazines, television, books and merchandise, under one corporate roof.

Going public changed how the business worked. An initial public offering lets outside investors buy shares that then trade on an exchange. After the October 1999 listing, any investor could own a stake in MSO. The company also took on the public financial reporting required of U.S.-listed firms.

The listing had a personal link to her past. Before she began catering from her home in Westport, Connecticut, in the 1970s, Stewart worked as a stockbroker. Her own company now had shares trading in the kind of market she once worked in.

MSO did not stay independent. Sequential Brands acquired Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia in 2015. The deal ended the company's run as a standalone public company trading as MSO.

That timeline gave the company about 18 years as an independent business, roughly 16 of them as a listed stock. The sale placed the company Stewart had founded under a new corporate owner. The name she had bought back from Time Warner in 1997 passed to Sequential Brands in 2015.

Martha Stewart Weddings launched in 1999 as a bridal magazine spun off from Martha Stewart Living. It took the Living brand's attention to food, flowers and hosting and applied it to one occasion: the wedding.

A spin-off let the company build on a name readers already knew. A new wedding title could start with the recognition of the Living brand rather than building an audience from nothing.

The subject also fit the hosting focus behind the brand. A wedding gathers many of those tasks into one event. Those include planning menus, arranging flowers, setting tables and timing a meal for a large group of guests.

The magazine format also suits the subject. A wedding is planned largely around how it will look, from the cake to the centerpieces. A print title can show those details in photographs that readers can keep and return to while they plan.

A bridal magazine serves a different reader than a general home title does. Someone interested in cooking or decorating may keep reading a home magazine for years. Someone planning a wedding has a fixed deadline. That need usually ends once the event is over.

That difference shapes how a bridal title works. Its audience turns over as each group of couples marries and a new group begins planning. A separate magazine can serve those readers without reshaping the main title around a single event.

Bridal publishing also connects readers with a specific set of businesses. Wedding planning involves decisions about dresses, rings, venues, invitations, flowers and gift registries. A magazine built around the wedding put the Martha Stewart brand in front of readers making those choices.

That concentration of purchases is what makes a bridal audience valuable to advertisers. A dressmaker, jeweler or stationer can reach readers at the moment they are deciding what to buy.

The title extended the Living brand into a new category of publishing while keeping its subject matter close to the brand's roots in entertaining.

Martha Stewart Living Radio was a satellite radio channel that launched on Sirius in the mid-2000s. It carried the Martha Stewart Living name into audio.

The channel arrived while Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia controlled the brand. For the company, radio became one more outlet for a name it already used across print, television and retail.

Sirius sold its service by subscription. Listeners paid for access to its full lineup of channels. They did not tune in free over the air, as they would with a local AM or FM station. A Martha Stewart channel on Sirius therefore reached people who had already chosen to pay for satellite programming.

Sirius built its lineup from many dedicated channels, each devoted to a single genre, topic or personality. That format gave a lifestyle brand room for a full channel of its own, rather than a short segment inside someone else's programming.

Satellite delivery also shaped where that audience could listen. Sirius programming reached listeners through dedicated receivers, many of them installed in cars. A satellite signal does not fade at the edge of a city's broadcast range, as a terrestrial station's signal does. That gave one channel national reach across the U.S.

Audio changed how the brand's material reached people. The magazine relied on photography. The television shows relied on on-screen demonstration. A radio channel had neither. Its content had to work through the spoken word alone.

That limit tested how much of the brand depended on visuals. A recipe or a cleaning method can be explained aloud, step by step. A table setting or a finished craft project is harder to convey without pictures.

The channel kept the Living title rather than introducing a new label. That choice tied the radio channel to the magazine and the syndicated show that shared the name. A listener who already knew one of them could recognize the others.

The Martha Stewart Collection is a home goods line that launched at Macy's $M in 2007. It followed her Kmart-era retail partnership and gave the Martha Stewart brand a new home in a U.S. department store chain.

The switch changed the kind of store carrying her name. Kmart was a discount chain built around low prices. Macy's is a department store, where home goods sit alongside clothing, beauty and other categories under one roof. That put the brand in front of a different set of shoppers.

The move also changed how the products were presented. In a discount store, a product competes mostly on price. In a department store, it sits in a setting built around browsing, displays and named labels. A line carrying her name fit that setting. Her name worked as the label.

The underlying model stayed the same. A national retailer sold a home goods range under her name through its own stores and staff. Her business did not need to open or run shops of its own to reach customers. The retailer handled store space, stock and checkout. Her side supplied the name and the brand identity behind it.

The Macy's line also connected to her media work. The Martha Stewart Show was on daytime television when the collection arrived. Her satellite radio channel was on the air in the same period. A viewer or listener who knew her name could then find it on department store goods.

The collection launched while Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia, the publicly traded company built around her brand, oversaw her media and merchandising businesses. That arrangement placed a department store line alongside the company's magazines, television programs and radio channel.

In that sense, the collection carried the brand's household subject matter from the page and the screen onto store shelves. The same name now covered what a reader learned and what a shopper bought.
