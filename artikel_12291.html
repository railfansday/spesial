Greatest Washington Senators: A Twinkie Town Definitive List (Round 2)

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

In the biggest landslide since Ronald Reagan in 1980, Walter Johnson was voted the greatest Washington Senator of all time. Had this been a poll for "greatest pitcher of all time", he'd still have gotten votes. Truly one of the all-time greats.

Up next: Another fleet-footed, defensive-whiz gamer.

Sam Rice (1915-1933): 2,889 H, 479 2B, 183 3B, 346 SB, .323 BA, .804 OPS, 113 OPS+, 54.1 bWAR. An outfielder who hit left-handed but threw right-handed, Rice was the ultimate speed-and-contact player for the Senators. After a stint in the U.S. Navy and a stop with the Petersburg Goobers of the Virginia League, he twice led the league in hits, once in stolen bases, and once in three-baggers. His career hit total—2,987—remains the closest to 3k of any retired player without reaching that milestone, a fact that may have kept him out of the HOF until the Veteran's Committee voted him aboard in 1963. Perhaps best known for a controversial catch in the 1925 World Series.

Goose Goslin (1921-1930, 1933, 1938): 1,659 H, 289 2B, 125 3B, 127 HR, 932 RBI, 117 SB, .323 BA, .888 OPS, 131 OPS+, 43.4 bWAR. A farm kid from New Jersey introduced to the sport via town ball, Leon Allen Goslin was given the nickname "Goose" when his penchant for waving his arms while tracking spheres in LF looked waterfowl-ish. Goose ultimately had the last laugh, winning an AL batting crown in 1928 and taking home the RBI distinction in 1924. His game was a mix of powerful contact and a little speed—twice leading the circuit in dashes to 3B. Four times he finished top-10 in AL MVP voting, and his six consecutive hits in the 1924 World Series helped the Senators nab their first title. The HOF Veteran's Committee inducted him in 1968. A year later, severe abdominal burns from falling asleep on his couch while smoking a cigar led to Goslin's larynx being removed and his death in 1971.

Joe Judge (1915-1932): 2,291 H, 421 2B, 157 3B, 210 SB, .299 BA, .802 OPS, 115 OPS+, 48.3 bWAR. As a batsman, Judge was functional and fleet. But in an era known for shoddy defense with bad baseballs and awkward hand-wear, Judge was a consummate glove-man at the first sack. At retirement, he led the American League in fielding percentage, putouts, assists, total chances, & double plays. The kid from Brooklyn—discovered by his block's postal worker, who gave young Joe his first glove—preserved Walter Johnson's only MLB no-hitter with a fantastic snare for the final out in 1920. Ironically, he effectively ended the Big Train's career in 1927 when he smashed a BP bottle-rocket into the hurler's shin and broke his ankle. Judge died at the age of 68 in 1963 while shoveling snow outside of his D.C. home.

Buddy Myer (1925-1927, 1929-1941): 1,828 H, 305 2B, 113 3B, 118 SB, .303 BA, .801 OPS, 109 OPS+, 42.3 bWAR. A college baseball star at Mississippi State, Myer was the Nick Punto of his era—bouncing around multiple infield positions as a proto-utility player. At the plate he was known for a keen eye, with OBPs routinely at .370+ (including five .400+ OBP years). Buddy's 1935 season was his apex, seeing him take home a batting title (.349), score & drive in 100 runs, and finish 4th in MVP voting to slugger Hank Greenberg. Never one to back down from a challenge, the diminutive (5'10") Myer once brawled with Ben Chapman (surprise, surprise) when Chapman spiked Myer and hurled antisemitic insults at him—leading to fans charging the field and multiple disorderly conduct arrests.

Clyde Milan (1907-1922): 2,100 H, 1,004 R, 495 SB, .285 BA, .706 OPS, 109 OPS+, 40.8 bWAR. Before Ty Cobb re-wrote the record books for speed on the base paths, Clyde "Deerfoot" Milan was the gold standard of stolen bases. Twice leading the American League in bags swiped (88 in 1912; 75 in 1913), Milan averaged 40 steals per season over his 16-year MLB career. What Clyde lacked in Dead Ball Era power numbers (17 career round-trippers) he made up for by choking up on the lumber and compiling a .353 career on-base percentage. In 1911-1913 he garnered three consecutive top-9 AL MVP finishes. Milan could man all three OF stations and often led the league in assists, double plays & Range Factor. A high error total speaks as much to Clyde's ability to even have a chance at suppressing more flying spheres than his contemporaries.
