I did some work for a california native silicon valley technical writer in the 80s. She did not understand what she was writing about, and had no way to ask the developers.

I can't speak for others, but I've often found I learn much faster by engaging with someone who's working on a project, than by sitting down and working through a tool's manual from cover to cover. Much like a course with lectures where I can ask questions and where the professor is attempting to present information calibrated to connect with her audience is a *much* better way to learn a subject than to just sit down alone with a textbook.

Developing a complete mastery of each tool *before* attempting to do anything with it is frequently not a viable approach, and mastery is often what is required in order to answer a question from the knowledge in a manual, as the answer may emerge from multiple small details spread across multiple chapters.

Meanwhile, there's probably someone out there who's working on the same exact problem you are, has stumbled in the same places, and now has an answer. And reading what they went through to *find* their answer can provide the clues to develop a better understanding of the tool, such that it's easier to find what you're looking for in the manual next time.

Manuals are good! But we're also not meant to exist and work in a vacuum. We're humans! We do better when we are in communication with each other.

Manuals are great if you know the API you want to call and need to know the details. They aren't good if you don't know the API, or even if one exists. Or if the problem you're debugging is more complex than just "passed the wrong parameter to this function".

Stack Overflow have built out a 'Stack Overflow For Agents' (SOFA).

It's at https://agents.stackoverflow.c... [stackoverflow.com]

But to sit on their new sofa, Stack Overflow has you tell your agent harness to add this 'skill' markdown file:         https://agents.stackoverflow.c... [stackoverflow.com].To me, it's obvious any coding agent's human-in-charge must check any 'skill' for incompetence and malice before asking their harness to obey it. But look at the length of that skill document! I gave up at the 25% mark. Plus, the skill is pointed at via a URL whose underlying document can change at any time. So you can't take the word of a friend who vetted the skill yesterday. Nor can you tell a new IDE to use the SOFA skill you vetted last week.

Imagine all the developer-time wasted manually auditing a constantly-shifting system of "skill" markdown files. I think a trusted GNU/EFF-like body is needed for the 'AI skills' era, providing where 'well-known', human-audited (and static!) 'approved' skills are stored. New skills incorporate these 'approved' skills by referring to the -well-known, adding modfications as required.

A similar concept exists with software licenses: GNU.org stores a static copy of the GPL 2.0. In a codebase, you can just glance at a LICENSE file to understand the licensing conditions. You can compare or diff the license file against the GPL 2.0 to verify. Some licenses add exceptions and carveouts, like the GPL v2.0 with the classpath exception: https://openjdk.org/legal/gplv... [openjdk.org]
