10 ways to prevent frozen pipes and thaw them safely

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

How to prevent frozen pipes with 10 practical steps, from insulating exposed lines and dripping faucets to thawing a frozen pipe safely without an open flame

A frozen pipe usually bursts because of pressure, not because water expands as it turns to ice. When ice blocks a pipe, water gets trapped between the blockage and a closed faucet. Pressure builds in that short section until the pipe gives way. That mechanism shapes the approach to winter plumbing. The goal is to keep water moving, keep cold air away from vulnerable lines and keep pressure from building up.

The risk is not spread evenly through a house. It concentrates in a handful of predictable places. These include pipes in attics, crawl spaces and garages, lines along exterior walls and the plumbing that feeds outdoor faucets. In these spots, pipes sit closest to outside air and farthest from the home's heat. A house can feel warm in every bedroom while a supply line behind a kitchen cabinet stays much colder.

Learning how to prevent frozen pipes is mostly a matter of preparation, and much of it costs little. Some steps take a few minutes before a cold snap, such as opening cabinet doors or disconnecting a garden hose. Others are small one-time projects. Fitting foam sleeves on exposed lines and sealing gaps where cables and vents enter the house both fall into that group. Electric heat cable adds active warmth for pipes that insulation alone cannot protect.

Prevention does not always work. The later slides in this list cover what to do when it fails. That starts with knowing where the main water shutoff valve is before anything goes wrong. Closing that valve quickly is the first step to limit damage when a pipe bursts.

Thawing a frozen pipe carries its own risks. The wrong heat source can start a fire or damage the pipe it is meant to save. The safe methods are slow and simple. They rely on household tools rather than flame. The final slide explains the signs that a frozen or damaged pipe has become a job for a plumber.

Each of the 10 slides below covers one step. Each can be read on its own, whether you are preparing for winter weeks in advance or dealing with a faucet that stopped running on a cold morning.

Letting a faucet drip during a hard freeze is one of the simplest ways to protect a plumbing line. A slow, steady trickle keeps water moving. It also gives the pressure inside the pipe a way out if ice starts to form.

The first benefit is movement. Still water sitting in a cold pipe gives ice time to build up along the walls and close off the line. Water that keeps flowing, even slowly, is harder to freeze. A drip does not make a pipe immune to extreme cold, but it buys time on the coldest nights.

The second benefit matters more for avoiding a burst pipe. A pipe rarely splits from the push of expanding ice alone. The usual culprit is water pressure. Once a plug of ice seals the line, water downstream of it is caught between the ice and the closed tap. Each bit of new ice squeezes that trapped water harder. In time the pipe wall fails under the strain.

This is why a pipe can rupture some distance from the frozen spot itself. The weak section is the length of line between the ice and the shut faucet, where the pressure builds. An open faucet breaks that trap. Even if a blockage develops, the water and pressure in that stretch can bleed off through the tap.

The faucets worth dripping are the ones fed by vulnerable lines. That usually means fixtures on exterior walls and any faucet served by pipes that run through unheated or exposed spaces. Fixtures on warm interior walls rarely need the same treatment.

A drip protects only the supply line it draws from. On a faucet with separate hot and cold handles, water left trickling from one handle does nothing for the line behind the other.

The drip does not need to be heavy. A thin, continuous stream is enough to keep water moving. A larger flow mostly adds to the water bill without adding much protection. The habit matters most overnight and during stretches when temperatures stay well below freezing. Exposed plumbing runs coldest at those times.

A dripping faucet also works as an early warning. If the trickle slows or stops on a cold night, ice may be forming somewhere along that line. That line needs attention before the problem gets worse.

Pipes in attics, crawl spaces, garages and exterior walls are the ones most likely to freeze, and insulation is a direct way to protect them. Foam pipe sleeves and fiberglass wrap both slow the loss of heat from the water inside.

These spaces share one weakness. They sit outside the heated part of a home, or right at its edge. On a cold night, the air around the pipes there can fall close to the outdoor temperature. A supply line in a vented crawl space or along the rafters of an unheated attic has little shielding from that cold. A pipe inside an exterior wall faces a similar problem, with only a thin layer of building material between it and the outside.

Foam sleeves are the usual choice for straight, reachable runs of pipe. Each sleeve is a foam tube slit along one side, which lets it be pressed over a pipe that is already installed. Sleeves come in different inside diameters. The right one fits snugly. A loose sleeve leaves an air gap around the pipe. Many sleeves have a self-sealing adhesive strip along the slit. Others need to be closed with tape. The joints where two sleeves meet should be taped as well, so no bare pipe shows at the seam.

Fiberglass wrap handles bends, valves and fittings, where a straight tube is hard to fit. It is wound around the pipe in overlapping layers. Many fiberglass wraps come with a foil or plastic facing, or are finished with tape, to keep moisture out. Wet fiberglass loses much of its insulating value. The fibers also irritate skin, eyes and lungs. Gloves, long sleeves, eye protection and a dust mask are standard gear when handling it.

Coverage matters as much as the material. Elbows, tees and short exposed stretches near the point where a pipe enters a wall are easy to skip. A gap of bare pipe is where freezing tends to start. Both hot and cold supply lines in an unheated space need covering. A hot water line holds standing water whenever no tap is running. Once that water cools, it can freeze too.

Insulation has a clear limit. It slows heat loss, but it does not generate heat. A wrapped pipe holds its warmth longer than a bare one, which buys time during a cold night. If the surrounding space stays below freezing long enough, the water inside will still cool and freeze. For that reason, insulation works best as one layer of protection rather than a complete fix on its own.

Inspect pipe insulation each fall before the first hard freeze. Rodents, moisture and repair work in an attic or crawl space can tear or dislodge sleeves and wrap. Any damaged section leaves that stretch of pipe exposed and should be replaced or retaped.

Closing the gaps where cables, dryer vents and pipes pass through exterior walls keeps freezing air away from nearby plumbing. Caulk or expanding foam seals most of these openings.

A pipe does not need to sit outdoors to freeze. A steady draft through a small opening can chill one section of a water line far below the temperature of the room around it. That cold spot is often where ice forms first, even in a home that feels warm.

The openings to check are the ones made when a house was wired, plumbed and vented. Cable and phone lines, outdoor faucet supply lines, dryer ducts and plumbing vents all pass through the building shell. Builders cut holes to fit them, and the space left around each one is rarely sealed perfectly. Over time, old caulk can dry out and crack, and gaps can widen.

Basements and crawl spaces deserve a close look. Water lines often run along the rim joist, the framing that rests on top of the foundation wall. Gaps there sit right next to the pipes. On a cold, windy day, holding a hand near a suspected gap or watching a thin strip of tissue for movement can show where air is getting in.

The right material depends on the size of the gap. Caulk suits narrow cracks and small seams. Expanding spray foam fills larger, irregular openings around pipes and cables. Low-expansion foam is sold for window and door frames, where heavy expansion can push framing out of shape.

Some openings need extra care. Seal only the space around a dryer duct, never the duct itself. The dryer has to vent its exhaust outside. Standard foam and caulk should not be used against chimneys or furnace flues. Those openings call for fire-rated sealants made for high heat.

An attached garage is another common source of cold air. Keeping the garage door closed during freezing weather limits the cold that reaches water lines running through the garage or along a shared wall.

Leaving the heat on at a consistent setting is a basic defense against frozen pipes. A thermostat held at the same temperature day and night keeps warmth moving into the walls, floors and cabinets where water lines run.

The reading on a thermostat reflects the air around the device itself. That device usually sits on an interior wall in a lived-in room. Pipes sit somewhere else. They run through exterior walls, under floors and along the edges of a house, where temperatures are lower than the number on the display. The setting on the wall is a starting point, not a measure of how cold the plumbing gets.

That gap matters most at night. Outdoor temperatures typically bottom out in the hours before dawn. That is also when many households program the heat to drop. A deep overnight setback during a cold snap removes heat at the moment pipes are most exposed. Holding the daytime setting through the night during a hard freeze uses more fuel, but it keeps a margin of warmth around the plumbing.

Trips away raise the same issue on a longer timeline. Turning the heat off or down sharply before a winter vacation can leave an empty house cooling for days. A commonly cited guideline is to keep the thermostat no lower than 55 degrees Fahrenheit while away. Treat that figure as a general floor rather than a guarantee. Older homes, drafty layouts and pipes in exterior walls may call for a higher setting.

A steady thermostat only helps if the heating system keeps running. Before a trip, check that the furnace or boiler is working. Confirm that any fuel supply, such as heating oil or propane, will last for the time away. Replace the thermostat batteries if the model uses them. A dead battery can stop the unit from calling for heat.

Asking a neighbor or friend to check on the house during an extended absence adds another layer of protection. A visitor can confirm the heat is still on and spot a problem before it spreads. Smart thermostats that send alerts or allow remote adjustment can serve a similar purpose when the home has a working internet connection.

Inside the house, keeping interior doors open lets warm air circulate into rooms that would otherwise stay cooler. Bedrooms, laundry rooms and bathrooms on outside walls are the rooms most likely to need that shared heat.

Opening the cabinet doors under sinks that sit against exterior walls lets warm room air reach the water pipes inside. The step requires no tools or materials.

The cabinet itself is the problem. A closed vanity or base cabinet forms a small, enclosed box. Its back panel often sits directly against an outside wall, where the wall cavity can get much colder than the room. Household heat has to pass through the cabinet doors to reach the pipes. Closed doors slow that exchange.

Several plumbing parts sit inside. A typical sink cabinet holds the hot and cold supply lines, their shutoff valves and the drain trap. Supply lines often enter through the back of the cabinet. On an exterior wall, that means water may run through one of the coldest parts of the house just before it reaches the faucet.

Kitchen sinks are frequent candidates. Many sit under a window on an outside wall. Bathroom vanities and laundry sinks on exterior walls carry the same exposure. Cabinets on interior walls are less vulnerable because the wall behind them stays closer to room temperature.

Leave the doors open whenever temperatures drop well below freezing, especially overnight, when outdoor temperatures usually reach their lowest point. Pull stored items away from the back of the cabinet so they do not block air from reaching the pipes. Packed rows of bottles, sponges and cleaning supplies can act as a barrier between the room and the plumbing.

That stored material is also the main safety concern. Under-sink cabinets commonly hold drain cleaner, bleach, dishwasher detergent and other household chemicals. In homes with young children or pets, move those products to a high shelf or a locked cabinet before propping the doors open. A child-safety latch offers no protection once the door is left standing open.

This step depends on the room being warm. An open cabinet in a heated kitchen exposes the pipes to that heat. An open cabinet in a cold, unheated room gives the pipes little added protection.

Remove every garden hose from its outdoor spigot before the first hard freeze. Then shut off the water to each spigot from inside the house and drain the line. Outdoor faucets sit on the outside face of the house wall, and the pipe feeding them is among the most exposed in the home.

An attached hose holds water. When that water freezes, the ice can back up from the hose into the faucet and into the pipe behind it. The faucet may look fine from outside while the damage sits inside the wall.

Start by unscrewing the hose and emptying it. Stretching it out on a slope or lifting one end lets the water run out. A drained hose can then be coiled and stored in a garage, shed or basement for the winter. Keeping it out of the cold also protects the hose itself from cracking.

Next, find the indoor shutoff valve for each outdoor faucet. It is usually on the same pipe, a short distance inside the wall. Look for it in a basement, crawl space or utility area. Close that valve. Then go outside and open the spigot so the water left in the line can drain out.

Some indoor valves have a small drain cap on the side. Opening it with a bucket underneath releases water trapped between the valve and the wall. Leave the outdoor faucet open through the winter. Any water that remains in the short section of pipe then has an open path out.

Frost-proof sillcocks offer extra protection, but not complete protection. These faucets have a long stem that places the actual valve inside the heated part of the house. When the handle is closed, water in the stem is meant to drain out through the spout. A hose left connected blocks that drainage. Water stays in the stem, freezes and can split the pipe.

A split in an outdoor faucet line often stays hidden until spring. The leak may only appear the first time someone turns the faucet on, sending water into the wall or basement. Watching the spigot and the indoor pipe behind it during that first use helps catch the problem early.

Electric pipe heating cable, often sold as heat tape, adds warmth directly to a pipe that would otherwise freeze. It is safe only when installed exactly as the manufacturer directs. The product must also be rated for the pipe material and for the location where it goes.

Heat cable fills a gap that other prevention steps leave open. Pipe insulation only slows heat loss. A cable connected to power produces its own heat. That makes it useful on lines in crawl spaces, unheated garages and other spots where the surrounding air stays below freezing for long stretches.

Two main designs are sold for home use. Self-regulating cable changes its heat output as the temperature around it changes. It gives off more warmth as the pipe gets colder. Constant-wattage cable produces the same amount of heat whenever it is on. Many constant-wattage versions rely on a built-in thermostat that switches the cable on as temperatures approach freezing.

The difference matters during installation. Constant-wattage cable generally must not cross over or overlap itself, because the doubled section can overheat. Self-regulating cable is often more forgiving on this point. Even so, the product's own instructions decide what is allowed, not the general category.

Pipe material is the next check. Some cables are approved for metal pipe only. Others are approved for plastic pipe, sometimes with a layer of aluminum tape required underneath to spread the heat evenly. Using a cable on a pipe it is not rated for can damage the pipe or create a fire risk.

Location ratings carry the same weight. A cable made for indoor use may not hold up outdoors or in damp areas. The manufacturer also sets whether insulation can be installed over the cable, and what type.

Choose a cable listed by a recognized testing laboratory, and plug it into an outlet with ground-fault circuit interrupter protection. Inspect it before each winter for cracks, discoloration or a brittle outer jacket. Replace a damaged cable rather than patching it.

Never improvise by wrapping an extension cord or other ordinary electrical cord around a pipe. Standard cord is not designed to heat anything. It lacks the temperature control and safety features built into pipe heating cable.

Locate your home's main water shutoff valve before temperatures drop, and confirm that it turns. If a frozen pipe splits, closing that valve quickly cuts off the supply and limits how much water reaches floors, walls and ceilings.

A burst pipe keeps releasing water for as long as the supply stays on. Every minute spent searching a dark basement for an unfamiliar valve is another minute of water spreading through the structure. Finding the valve on a calm afternoon removes that delay.

The main shutoff usually sits close to the point where the water service line enters the home. In houses with basements, that is often on a foundation wall facing the street. In homes built on a slab or over a crawl space, it may be in a utility closet, a garage or the crawl space itself. It can also sit near the water heater. Some homes in warmer climates have the valve outdoors. If the home has an indoor water meter, the main valve is commonly beside it.

Two types of valve are common. A gate valve typically has a round wheel handle that turns clockwise to close and may need several full rotations. A ball valve has a lever handle that closes with a quarter turn. When it is closed, the lever sits perpendicular to the pipe. Knowing which type is installed tells you what the closed position looks like.

Test the valve before you need it. Older wheel valves can seize after years without use. Forcing a stuck handle during an emergency can break it. If the valve will not move, or leaks around the stem after turning, have it repaired or replaced while there is no crisis.

Many homes also have a second shutoff at the street, often inside a meter box near the curb. That valve generally belongs to the water utility and may require a special key. Check the local utility's rules before operating it.

Once the main valve is closed, open the faucets in the house to drain the water left in the lines. Start with a faucet on the top floor and finish with one on the lowest level. That order lets air in from above and lets water run out below. Flushing the toilets empties their tanks as well.

The same steps help when a home will sit empty during a cold stretch. Closing the main and draining the lines leaves less water in pipes that could freeze while no one is there to notice.

Label the valve with a tag, and show everyone in the household where it is and which way it turns.

Thaw a frozen pipe by opening the faucet it feeds, then applying gentle heat with a hair dryer, a heating pad or warm towels. Start closest to the faucet and work back toward the blockage. Never use an open flame.

The first clue usually shows up at the tap. A faucet that delivers only a trickle, or nothing at all, during freezing weather often sits on a line with ice inside it. The frozen stretch tends to be somewhere exposed to cold air. Pipes along an outside wall or through an unheated area are common culprits.

Open that faucet before any heat goes on. As the ice softens, the melted water needs somewhere to go. An open tap gives it an exit. Once water starts moving through the pipe, the flow also helps melt the remaining ice.

Begin heating at the faucet end of the pipe. Move gradually toward the frozen section from there. Working in this direction lets water from the thawed portion drain out through the open tap rather than collecting behind the ice.

Three common household items handle the job. A hair dryer can be passed back and forth along the pipe. An electric heating pad can be wrapped around it. Towels soaked in hot water and wrapped around the pipe add heat without any electricity. Each delivers gradual warmth rather than intense, concentrated heat. Keep applying heat until full water pressure returns at the tap.

Open flame is off the list entirely. That includes propane torches, blowtorches, kerosene heaters and charcoal stoves. A flame near a pipe can ignite wood framing, insulation or other building materials around it. Intense heat can also damage the pipe itself or cause it to burst.

Electric tools bring their own hazard. Thawing ice produces water, and a weakened pipe can leave puddles on the floor. Keep the hair dryer, the heating pad and their cords away from any standing water.

Once one pipe thaws, check the other faucets in the home. The same cold that blocked one line can freeze others in similar locations.

Call a plumber if you cannot find the frozen section of pipe, cannot reach it or cannot restore flow with gentle heat. Call one right away if you see a crack or a leak. If a pipe bursts, shut off the main water valve first and keep it off until the pipe is repaired.

Finding the frozen section is often the hardest part. It may sit inside an exterior wall, above a ceiling or under a floor. Reaching it can mean cutting into drywall or crawling into a tight space under the house. Opening a wall or floor to get at a pipe is repair work, not a household thaw.

Check every faucet in the house before treating the problem as solved. Pipes near a frozen line may also be cold enough to freeze. A single line can also freeze in more than one spot. If several faucets produce nothing, the blockage may be on a pipe that supplies a larger part of the house. That kind of blockage is harder to trace.

Gentle heat has limits. If a pipe stays blocked after a sustained, careful effort, stop and call a plumber rather than turning to stronger heat.

A crack may not show while the pipe is still frozen. Ice can plug a split, and water escapes only once the ice melts. Watch the pipe closely as it thaws. Look for drips, spraying water, damp patches or stains spreading across a ceiling or wall.

The water meter can also reveal a hidden leak. Turn off every faucet and every appliance that uses water. If the meter keeps moving, water is escaping somewhere in the system.

If a pipe bursts, close the main water valve at once and then call a plumber. Do not turn the water back on until the pipe is repaired. Restoring pressure to a damaged line pushes more water into walls, floors and ceilings.

Keep water away from outlets, appliances and the electrical panel. If water has reached any of them, stay out of the wet area. Shut off power at the panel only if you can reach it without standing in water.

While you wait for the repair, move furniture and belongings out of the wet area and soak up standing water. Photos of the damage and the failed pipe create a record of what happened.
