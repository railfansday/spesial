Building Community Takes More Than an Invitation

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Posted November 1, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

I’ve been reading The Middle of Somewhere: Rural Education Partnerships and Innovation, edited by Hartman and Klein, a book about rural school communities and partnerships. As I read, I keep thinking about the energy it takes to build a community. It involves learning what matters to people, finding ways to contribute, and being willing to receive what others offer. Those ideas stayed with me while watching Hallmark’s Who’s Coming for Christmas? The main character's wish to fill her Christmas table raises a question that extends well beyond the holidays: How do we create a community when we are still trying to find our place in it?

Julie just moved to Pine Tree Lane. She is learning a new job as the head librarian, getting to know her neighbors, and imagining what her life might become. After meeting a neighbor, who is a divorced father, and his daughter, a voracious reader who also writes poetry, she explains her dream of having a full table for Christmas.

Two of the dining room table seats were to be held for Julie’s parents, but they won a Christmas cruise. Facing an empty table in a town where she’s starting over, she begins to doubt that she’ll have anyone at her table for Christmas. She must decide to build her community by meeting new people who will fill her table or put aside her dream until next year and dine alone. Mark agrees to introduce her to community members, sold on her optimism about meeting eight people to fill her Christmas table.

Julie’s dining room table represents more than a place to eat. It represents something many of us want when we move or experience a major transition: people with whom to share our lives. We may know where to buy groceries, get a haircut, and how to get to work long before we know whom to invite over or call when we need company. Julie approaches that uncertainty with optimism and vulnerability. She needs to both let people know that she wants to spend time with them and hope that they would like that, too.

The Social Identity Model of Identity Change offers a way to understand what both Julie and Mark are building. This model suggests that maintaining meaningful group connections and developing new ones can help us adjust during life transitions. Julie’s grandmother’s table symbolizes continuity with her family, while her invitations create opportunities to become part of a new community. Mark is maintaining his connection with Vanessa while exploring how their holiday traditions and local relationships can grow following his divorce. Viewed through this lens, both characters are finding ways to carry meaningful parts of their lives into a future that includes new sources of belonging.

Julie is also building community through her work at the library, especially among young readers. Books offer a starting point for connection. A shared interest can open a conversation, and returning to a welcoming space can give people opportunities to become familiar with one another.

Mark’s daughter, Vanessa, loves reading and writing. Her interests offer a way for Julie to connect with her as a person with ideas and stories worth hearing. That attention matters. Creating community involves learning who people are and making room for what they bring. In fact, Vanessa is the first to share her poetry at the Christmas Voices event Julie organizes to increase interest at the library.

Building community also involves receiving. We may find it comfortable to offer help, organize an event, or invite someone to dinner. Accepting help can feel more exposing. It means acknowledging that we have needs, sharing those needs with others, and allowing someone else to see those needs and respond. Whether we’re new to a community or building a new community of friends, this can be challenging. For Julie, building community means allowing other people to help shape the life she is creating.

Julie begins to believe that her table will be full in time for Christmas and can’t help but feel disappointed as people begin cancelling. An invitation can seem like a simple gesture until we wait for an answer or see those plans unravel. All of a sudden, a simple invitation becomes much bigger than spending an evening together.

For someone establishing new relationships, a cancellation may have us asking uncomfortable questions. Did I misunderstand our connection? Was I too eager? Do I matter to these people? Those feelings can arise even when the cancellation has a reasonable explanation. In Julie’s case, weather disrupts plans. Disappointment is real, but it does not necessarily tell us how much others care.

Nurturing community requires willingness to remain open through this uncertainty. That does not mean repeatedly pursuing people who show no interest. It means leaving room for relationships to develop without asking each interaction to prove that we belong.

Hallmark: A Christmas table offers a visible, satisfying picture of people coming together.

Psychology: The relationships around that table depend on what happens beyond the celebration: paying attention, following through, accepting help, and reaching out again.

The Verdict: Hallmark is on the right track but misses the full range of community building. A full table is one possible expression of community. A smaller gathering can hold meaningful connection, too.

The book I’m reading has prompted me to think about how much participation community asks of us. Julie and Mark remind me that this includes the willingness to be new, to need people, and to make an invitation without knowing exactly how it will be received.
