How to spot carbon monoxide poisoning and keep your home safe this winter

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Carbon monoxide poisoning symptoms and prevention steps explained, from flu-like warning signs to alarm placement, generator safety and what to do if an alarm sounds this winter

Carbon monoxide is a poison the human body has no way to sense. A person cannot see it, smell it or taste it. It gives no warning before it starts to cause harm. That makes protection a matter of equipment and habits rather than instinct.

The gas forms whenever a fuel burns without enough oxygen to burn completely. Natural gas, propane, oil, wood, charcoal and gasoline can all produce it. A furnace, a water heater, a fireplace, a portable generator and a car engine are all possible sources inside or next to a home.

Winter concentrates those sources. Furnaces, boilers and fireplaces run for hours at a time when temperatures drop. Windows stay shut and doors stay closed to hold in heat. Less fresh air moves through the house as a result. Snow can pile up against exterior vents and cover tailpipes. Storms that knock out power push households toward portable generators, camp stoves and grills — equipment built to run outdoors, not in a garage or living room.

The early effects are easy to misread. A dull headache, dizziness or nausea on a cold evening can look like a seasonal bug. People who assume they are coming down with something often stay home to rest. That keeps them in the same air that is making them sick.

A working grasp of carbon monoxide poisoning symptoms and prevention comes down to a few practical habits. One is knowing how the warning signs differ from an ordinary illness. Another is putting working CO alarms in the right places and checking them on a schedule. The rest involves keeping fuel-burning equipment maintained, properly vented and used only where it was designed to operate.

The 10 slides that follow cover a selection of key points for the cold months. They begin with how poisoning shows up in the body and why the gas is so hard to notice. They then move through detector placement and testing, generator safety, grills and stoves, annual appliance servicing, blocked vents and chimneys, and car exhaust in garages. The final slide explains what to do if an alarm goes off or someone in the home starts to feel unwell.

Headache, dizziness, weakness and an upset stomach are the most common early signs of carbon monoxide poisoning. As exposure continues, vomiting, chest pain and confusion can follow.

These complaints overlap heavily with a winter cold or flu. Temperature is one useful difference. The flu usually brings a fever, while carbon monoxide poisoning typically does not. A household full of flu-like symptoms with normal thermometer readings deserves a second look.

Mistaking these signs for an ordinary illness is what makes the early stage risky. A person who blames a headache on a virus has no reason to leave the building. Every extra hour in contaminated air prolongs the exposure.

Confusion adds another layer of risk. Someone who is thinking less clearly may not connect the symptoms to their surroundings. They may also find it harder to decide to leave. That makes the earliest signs the best moment to act.

The most useful clue is a pattern rather than any single symptom. A virus tends to pass from one person to the next over a period of days. Carbon monoxide affects everyone breathing the same air at roughly the same time. Several people in one home feeling unwell at once points toward a shared environmental cause.

Pets offer a similar signal. A dog or cat that appears sick alongside its owners is breathing the same indoor air. Illness that shows up in people and animals in one home at the same moment is a reason for suspicion.

Location and timing provide the second clue. Symptoms from carbon monoxide often ease after a person leaves the building. They can return once that person comes back inside. Someone who feels fine at work or school but gets a headache each evening at home has a pattern worth noting.

The onset of symptoms can also point to a source. Symptoms that begin soon after the furnace starts up for the season, or that feel worst near a fireplace or other fuel-burning appliance, suggest a household cause rather than an infection.

Treating a sudden, shared, fever-free illness as a possible exposure rather than a bug gives a household the chance to get out while symptoms are still mild.

Carbon monoxide has no color, no odor and no taste, so a person cannot sense it building up in a room. It is a product of incomplete combustion.

Complete combustion turns the carbon in fuel into carbon dioxide. That process needs a steady supply of oxygen. A flame may be starved of air, poorly adjusted or burning in a cramped space. In those conditions, some carbon bonds with only one oxygen atom instead of two. The result is carbon monoxide, often shortened to CO.

Nothing about the gas gives it away. It does not sting the eyes or irritate the throat the way smoke does. It produces no haze. The rotten-egg smell many people associate with a gas problem comes from an odorant that utilities add to natural gas. Carbon monoxide carries no such warning. A home can smell normal while CO levels climb.

Once inhaled, the gas passes from the lungs into the bloodstream. There it binds to hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Hemoglobin takes up carbon monoxide more readily than it takes up oxygen. Each binding site occupied by CO is one that cannot deliver oxygen to the body's tissues.

The heart and brain are especially sensitive to this shortage. Both organs need a constant oxygen supply to function. As exposure continues, less oxygen reaches them. That oxygen deprivation is what makes carbon monoxide poisoning dangerous and, in serious cases, fatal.

With no sensory cue to wake them, people who are asleep may have no chance to react. The same is true for anyone intoxicated by alcohol or drugs. Both groups can lose consciousness or die before they recognize that anything is wrong. Exposure during the night, when a household is asleep, carries particular danger for this reason.

Some people face higher risk from the same exposure. Infants and older adults are more vulnerable. So are people with chronic heart disease, anemia or breathing problems. Each of those conditions already limits how much oxygen the body can take in or deliver. That leaves less margin when carbon monoxide displaces oxygen in the blood.

Carbon monoxide alarms belong on every level of a home and outside each separate sleeping area. A home with bedrooms on two floors, or a bedroom wing set apart from the main living space, needs coverage in each of those places.

The sleeping-area rule exists because people are most vulnerable at night, when symptoms go unnoticed. An alarm in the hallway just outside the bedrooms sits where it can wake the people sleeping there. Where bedrooms sit at opposite ends of a house, one hallway alarm may not cover both. A closed bedroom door can also muffle an alarm mounted farther away.

The every-level rule covers the places where fuel-burning equipment tends to sit. Furnaces, boilers and water heaters are often in basements or utility rooms, out of sight and far from bedrooms. A basement counts as a level and needs its own alarm, as does a finished attic or upper floor.

Smoke alarms do not detect carbon monoxide. A house fitted with smoke alarms in every room can still have no protection against CO. Combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are available and can handle both jobs from one mounting point. The label or packaging states which hazards a device senses. Check it rather than assume.

Mounting height and exact position vary by model. The manufacturer's instructions set those details, which may include how far the unit should sit from appliances. Those instructions are the rule to follow. Do not place an alarm directly next to a furnace, water heater, stove or other fuel-burning appliance.

An attached garage creates a separate concern. Car exhaust can seep from the garage through the shared wall or the connecting door into the house. Placing an alarm in the living space next to an attached garage gives warning if that happens. That room is often a kitchen, mudroom or hallway. Plan coverage there even if another alarm already sits elsewhere on the same floor.

Press the test button on every carbon monoxide alarm regularly, change its batteries as the manufacturer directs and replace the whole unit by the date printed on it. A CO alarm that is dead, expired or untested offers no protection.

The test button is the quickest check. Holding it down should set off the alarm's horn. That confirms the device has power and can make a sound loud enough to hear. On many models, the button checks the battery, circuitry and sounder rather than exposing the sensor to carbon monoxide gas. That is one reason the expiration date matters as much as a passing test.

Never try to test a detector with car exhaust, a lit grill or any other real source of carbon monoxide. Doing so puts the person holding the alarm at risk. The test button and the steps in the manufacturer's manual are the methods a homeowner should use.

Carbon monoxide alarms wear out. The sensor inside degrades over time, and an aging unit may stop responding reliably even if its lights still come on. Manufacturers set a service life for each model and print the replacement date on the unit itself. That date often appears on a label on the back or side. Lifespans differ between brands and models, so the label is the figure to trust, not a general rule of thumb. When a combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarm reaches its date, the entire unit needs replacing.

Batteries need their own attention. Some alarms run on standard replaceable batteries. Others plug into an outlet or are hardwired, with a battery as backup during a power cut. Some models have a sealed battery meant to last the life of the unit, and those are replaced whole. The manual explains which type a home has and how often to change the battery.

The sound an alarm makes also carries information. An intermittent chirp usually signals a low battery or an alarm that has reached the end of its life. A full, continuous alarm is different. It means the device has detected carbon monoxide at a dangerous level. Treat that sound as an emergency, not a malfunction. Exact sound patterns vary by manufacturer, and many units print a guide on the label.

Linking checks to a fixed point on the calendar makes them easier to remember. One option is the start of the heating season. Another is the weekend the clocks change in places that observe daylight saving time. Checking the printed replacement date at the same time catches an expired unit before winter sets in.

A portable generator must never run inside a home, garage, basement or crawlspace. Opening doors or windows does not make indoor use safe. The machine belongs outdoors every time it runs.

A portable generator is driven by a small fuel-burning engine. Like a car engine, it burns fuel and pushes out exhaust as it runs. That exhaust contains carbon monoxide. In an enclosed or partly enclosed space, the gas builds up faster than outside air can replace it.

An open door or window changes little. Air does not move through a building in a steady stream that carries exhaust away. The gas collects in rooms and corners instead. A garage with its door raised still has three walls and a ceiling to hold it in. Fans do not prevent the buildup either.

The CDC advises setting a generator up outdoors, well away from windows, doors and vents. That distance keeps exhaust from drifting back into the house. A unit parked just outside a kitchen window or beside an air intake can still send carbon monoxide indoors. Wind can also push exhaust toward an opening the operator did not consider. The owner's manual for each model gives the manufacturer's own placement guidance.

Winter storms are a common time for generator-related poisonings. Ice and heavy snow bring down power lines, and some outages last for days. Households turn to a generator to keep a furnace, refrigerator or lights running. Cold, wind and snow make working outdoors harder. That pressure leads some people to run the unit in a garage, a shed or an enclosed porch.

Planning before a storm removes the need to improvise during one. Choose an outdoor spot ahead of the season, on level ground and clear of the home's doors, windows and vents. Keep a path to it shoveled when snow falls. Some manufacturers make covers or tents that shield a generator from rain and snow. Use only one the manufacturer approves for that model, and never one that encloses the engine.

Outages also affect the alarms meant to catch a problem. A carbon monoxide alarm that plugs into a wall outlet with no battery backup stops working when the power fails. A battery-powered or battery-backup unit keeps working during the hours a generator is most likely to be running.

Charcoal grills, gas grills, camp stoves and propane heaters built for outdoor use should never run inside a house, tent, garage or camper. Each one releases carbon monoxide as it burns, and an enclosed space traps that gas.

A grill on the patio or a camp stove in the hall closet can look like a quick fix for a cold house or a meal without electricity. Bringing that equipment inside turns it into a CO source with nowhere for its exhaust to go.

Outdoor cooking gear is designed on the assumption that open air will carry combustion gases away. Indoors, the exhaust stays in the room. Burning fuel also draws down the oxygen in a closed space. As oxygen falls, the flame burns less cleanly and gives off more carbon monoxide.

Charcoal carries its own hazard. Briquettes keep giving off carbon monoxide after the flames die down and the coals are only glowing. A grill that looks finished can still be releasing gas. That rules out moving a cooling grill into a garage, an enclosed porch or a tent to use its leftover warmth. The safer step is to let it burn out and cool completely outside, well away from the house.

Tents, campers and small cabins hold far less air than a house. Carbon monoxide can reach harmful levels in them faster as a result. Camp stoves and grills belong outside the shelter, including on cold nights at a campsite or during a winter cabin trip.

Portable propane heaters differ by design. Some models are labeled for indoor use, and others are made for outdoor use only. The product label and the manufacturer's instructions set where a heater can operate. A unit marked for outdoor use stays outdoors, whatever the temperature.

The kitchen stove is the household version of the same mistake. Gas ovens and stovetops are built for cooking, not for heating a home. Leaving the oven door open or running burners for warmth keeps a combustion appliance going far longer than intended. Its exhaust then spreads through the living space.

During an outage, warmer clothing, extra blankets and closing off unused rooms help hold heat without adding a new fuel source. A public warming center or the home of a friend with power is another option.

The CDC recommends having a qualified technician inspect the furnace, boiler, water heater, gas dryer, fireplace and any other fuel-burning appliance in the home every year. That visit is best scheduled before the heating season begins.

A yearly check matters because carbon monoxide output depends on how well an appliance burns its fuel. Gas, oil and wood need the right balance of fuel and air to burn cleanly. When that balance slips, combustion becomes incomplete. The appliance then produces more CO than it was designed to.

A poorly adjusted burner is one common cause. If a burner receives too little air, the flame cannot convert all of the fuel into carbon dioxide and water vapor. Some of the carbon leaves as carbon monoxide instead. The problem can develop slowly as parts wear or shift. It may bring no obvious change in how the furnace sounds or heats.

A cracked heat exchanger is another fault a technician looks for. In a forced-air furnace, the heat exchanger keeps the burning fuel and its exhaust separate from the air the blower pushes through the ducts. A crack can let combustion gases mix with that household air. The metal heats and cools through many cycles each winter. That repeated stress can open cracks over time.

A faulty connection is the third common problem. Vent pipes, joints and fittings carry exhaust from the appliance to the outdoors. A loose, corroded or separated connection can release exhaust into a basement, utility room or closet before it reaches the flue.

During a typical service call, a technician examines the burners and flame and inspects the heat exchanger. The technician also checks the venting and its connections for leaks or damage. The visit may include a check of how the appliance drafts. That test shows whether exhaust is moving up and out as intended.

Scheduling the inspection in late summer or early fall leaves time for repairs before cold weather arrives. A furnace or boiler that sat idle for months will soon run for long stretches in a closed-up house. Water heaters and gas dryers operate all year, and they belong on the same annual schedule.

A blocked chimney, flue or appliance vent can push exhaust, including carbon monoxide, back into the home. Keep every vent open, and check the exterior ones after each heavy snowfall.

Fuel-burning appliances depend on a clear path to the outdoors. A furnace, boiler or water heater sends its combustion gases out through a flue or vent pipe. A fireplace relies on the chimney. When that path narrows or closes, the gases have nowhere to go. They spill back through the appliance and into the rooms where people breathe.

Three kinds of blockage are common. Snow and ice can bury vent openings on the outside of a house. Leaves, twigs and other debris can collect in chimneys and vent caps. Birds, squirrels and other animals can build nests inside chimneys and flues. A chimney that sat unused through the warmer months is especially likely to hold one.

Snow is a particular risk for high-efficiency furnaces and water heaters. Many of these appliances vent through plastic pipes that exit a side wall rather than the roof. Those openings often sit low on the house, where drifting or plowed snow can cover them. After a storm, walk around the house and find the exhaust and intake pipes for the furnace and water heater. Clear the snow around them by hand or with a brush. A shovel blade could crack the pipe.

The dryer vent needs the same check. A gas dryer burns fuel, and its exhaust leaves through the same duct that carries lint outside. Lint, snow or a stuck exterior flap can all restrict that flow. Make sure the outdoor flap opens freely when the dryer runs.

Openings at roof level matter as well. A chimney cap with mesh sides helps keep animals and debris out of the flue. Before the first fire of the season, have the chimney inspected and cleaned as needed. A chimney sweep can spot nests, soot buildup and liner damage that cannot be seen from the hearth.

Heavy snow on a roof can also cover short vent stacks. If a roof vent is buried, do not climb onto an icy roof to reach it. Call a roofing contractor or the appliance's service company instead.

Never leave a car running inside a garage, even with the garage door wide open. Carbon monoxide from the exhaust can build up in the enclosed space and seep into an attached home.

A gasoline engine produces carbon monoxide whenever it runs. Combustion inside the engine is never complete. Some of the fuel's carbon leaves the tailpipe as CO rather than carbon dioxide. Outdoors, that exhaust disperses into open air. Inside a garage, the walls and ceiling hold it close to the car.

An open garage door does not solve the problem. A single opening gives exhaust no reliable path out, and still air does little to carry it away. Garages attached to a house share walls and doors with the living space, and sometimes ductwork. Gaps around an interior door and openings around pipes or wiring give the gas a route indoors. People inside the home may not realize a car is idling on the other side of the wall.

The safer habit on a cold morning is to back the car out of the garage first. Then let it warm up in the driveway. Close the garage door behind it so exhaust does not drift back in. The same logic applies to a remote starter, which should never be triggered while the car is parked inside.

Snow creates a second hazard outside the garage. A tailpipe packed with snow can push exhaust back under the car and into the cabin, where the driver and passengers breathe it. Before starting the engine after a storm, clear the tailpipe and the area around it. This check matters when a plow leaves a ridge of snow behind a parked car. It also matters when a car is stuck in a drift.

Keyless-ignition vehicles add a quieter risk. Without a physical key to pull out, a driver can walk away from a car that is still running. A modern engine can idle quietly enough to go unnoticed in a closed garage. Before leaving the vehicle, check the dashboard to confirm the engine is off.

If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds or anyone suspects poisoning, get every person and pet out of the building and into fresh air right away. Then call 911 or the local emergency number from outside.

Speed matters more than diagnosis. There is no way to confirm or rule out a problem by walking through the house and checking rooms. Treat every full alarm as real until trained responders say otherwise.

Leave without stopping to hunt for the source, switch off appliances room by room or gather belongings. Each extra minute indoors means more exposure. Help anyone who cannot move on their own, such as small children, if it can be done quickly. Bring pets out as well. They breathe the same air as everyone else in the home.

Opening windows and staying inside is not a substitute for leaving. A few open windows do not reliably clear the gas from the whole house. Whatever is producing it may also still be running.

Make the emergency call from the yard, a neighbor's home or a spot well outside the building, not from a hallway or doorway. Tell the dispatcher that a carbon monoxide alarm went off and how many people were inside. Mention whether anyone feels unwell. If someone is still in the building, say so immediately. That lets crews reach them first.

Do not go back inside until emergency responders say it is safe. Fire crews can measure carbon monoxide levels in the air and look for the source. Going back in early to grab a phone or check on an appliance only adds to the exposure.

Anyone with symptoms needs a medical evaluation, even if they start to feel better outside. Feeling better in fresh air does not mean the gas has cleared from the body. Tell the paramedic or doctor that carbon monoxide exposure is suspected. Its warning signs can resemble other common illnesses.

The standard treatment is oxygen, usually given through a mask. Breathing concentrated oxygen helps clear carbon monoxide from the blood faster than ordinary air does. In severe cases, doctors may use hyperbaric oxygen therapy. In that treatment, the patient breathes oxygen inside a pressurized chamber.

After responders clear the home, do not restart the furnace, generator, vehicle or other equipment suspected of causing the problem. Have a qualified technician inspect it first. A repair at that stage keeps the same fault from causing a second emergency.
