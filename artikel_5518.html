Tornadoes by state: 10 U.S. states ranked by yearly average, Texas to Minnesota

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Tornadoes by state, from Texas's yearly average of 124 to Minnesota's 46: 10 U.S. states ranked by Storm Prediction Center data for 2003 to 2022, with notable storms

Texas averaged 124 tornadoes a year from 2003 to 2022, the highest figure among the 48 states in a ranking built on Storm Prediction Center data. Kansas placed second at 87. That puts Texas 37 tornadoes a year ahead of its nearest rival.

Tornadoes by state vary widely, and the yearly averages for that 20-year window show where the storms touch down most often. Several states share a rank because of ties. This piece covers 10 of them, running from Texas down to Florida and Minnesota, which tie for ninth at 46 tornadoes a year. Louisiana and Nebraska come next in the full table at 45.

A yearly average measures how often tornadoes strike, not how strong they are. Strength is rated on the Enhanced Fujita scale, or EF scale, which sorts tornadoes by wind speed. EF0 covers winds of 65 to 85 mph, and EF1 runs from 86 to 110 mph. EF2 spans 111 to 135 mph, and EF3 covers 136 to 165 mph. EF4 storms reach 166 to 200 mph. Anything above 200 mph is an EF5.

Tornado activity peaks in May and June. Individual states run on their own calendars. In Missouri, the peak stretches from April through June, though a tornado can strike there in any month. Iowa's activity peaks from May through July.

Terrain and air masses shape each state's count. Kansas sits in the middle of Tornado Alley, where flat land offers few natural features to slow a storm. The pattern shifts farther east. In Alabama, the Tennessee Valley and the Appalachian foothills tend to funnel storms. Flat, open farmland in northern and central Mississippi favors long-lived tornadoes. In Missouri, warm, humid Gulf air collides with cooler air from the Rockies and the upper Midwest.

Each slide gives a state's rank and yearly average, then the conditions behind its count. It also covers storms that shaped each state's history, from the 1925 Tri-State Tornado in Illinois, the deadliest in U.S. history, to the 2011 Joplin EF5 in Missouri.

Texas records more tornadoes than any other state, averaging 124 a year from 2003 to 2022. That figure comes from Storm Prediction Center data compiled in a state ranking by WorldAtlas.

Spread across a calendar, an average of 124 works out to more than two tornadoes a week.

WorldAtlas links much of the Texas total to the state's size. Its territory covers very different environments. Flat plains dominate the west, while the east is humid and forested.

Tornadoes do not fall evenly across that territory. WorldAtlas places most of the activity in two regions. One is the Panhandle, the northernmost part of the state. The other is northeastern Texas.

Large cities sit within reach of these storms. Dallas, Fort Worth and Houston have each experienced damaging tornadoes. Dallas and Fort Worth are in north Texas.

Houston sits on the upper Texas coast, outside both of the regions WorldAtlas names. Its record shows that damaging tornadoes in Texas are not limited to the areas where they are most common.

WorldAtlas highlights the 1953 Waco tornado. It killed 114 people and injured about 600 more.

The storm also damaged around 1,600 buildings and an estimated 2,000 vehicles.

Kansas averages 87 tornadoes a year, the second-highest count of any state in Storm Prediction Center data for 2003 to 2022. Only Texas records more.

Kansas logs about 70% as many tornadoes a year as Texas. Texas also spans several times as much land. The Kansas total is packed into a far smaller area.

Kansas runs 21 tornadoes a year ahead of Oklahoma, which ranks third at 66. Missouri, its neighbor to the east, averages 53 a year, or 34 fewer than Kansas.

The 87 figure is an average. It describes a typical year across two decades of data rather than any single season. Individual years can land above or below it.

WorldAtlas places Kansas in the middle of Tornado Alley. Within the state, tornado activity concentrates in the central and western regions.

The land across those regions is flat. Few natural features stand in a storm's path to slow it down, WorldAtlas notes.

Kansans prepare for that exposure. Warning systems are common across the state, and so are storm shelters. Home safe rooms are common as well. A safe room is a reinforced room in a home, built to shield the people in it from a tornado's winds.

In 2007, a tornado struck the city of Greensburg, Kansas. It destroyed 95% of the city. Destruction on that scale left little of Greensburg standing.

Oklahoma ranks third among U.S. states for tornadoes, with an average of 66 a year from 2003 to 2022. Most of them touch down in the central and northern parts of the state, with the heaviest concentration along the Interstate 35 corridor.

Interstate 35 crosses Oklahoma from the Texas line to the Kansas line. It passes through Oklahoma City, the state capital and largest city, along the way. The state's busiest tornado zone therefore follows one of its main north-south routes.

The only two states with higher yearly averages border Oklahoma: Texas to the south and Kansas to the north. Mississippi follows in fourth place at 64 a year, two fewer than Oklahoma.

Tornadoes in Oklahoma usually arrive in clusters during spring outbreaks rather than one at a time.

WorldAtlas notes that Oklahoma sees many EF4 and EF5 tornadoes, the two most violent ratings on the Enhanced Fujita scale.

Oklahoma City and Tulsa are both growing urban centers. Tulsa, the state's second-largest city, sits in the northeast, away from Interstate 35.

The Woodward $WWD tornado of April 9, 1947, remains one of the deadliest in Oklahoma's history. It struck the town of Woodward in the northwestern part of the state. The storm killed 116 people and injured 782.

Woodward lies well west of Interstate 35. Deadly tornadoes in Oklahoma have struck outside the corridor as well.

WorldAtlas credits the Bridge Creek-Moore tornado with winds of 321 mph. It describes that figure as the highest wind speed ever recorded on Earth. The storm struck on May 3, 1999. Its reported speed sits more than 100 mph above the threshold for an EF5 rating.

Bridge Creek, the community named first, lies southwest of Oklahoma City in Grady County. Moore is a suburb just south of Oklahoma City on Interstate 35. The storm's path ran through the same central corridor where the state's tornado activity is most concentrated.

Mississippi averages 64 tornadoes a year, the fourth-highest count among U.S. states in Storm Prediction Center data for 2003 to 2022. Its most severe tornadoes tend to strike the northern and central parts of the state.

A narrow margin separates Mississippi from the states ranked on either side of it. Oklahoma, in third place, averages two more tornadoes a year. Alabama, Mississippi's eastern neighbor, averages one fewer and ranks fifth.

Terrain helps explain where the worst storms occur. Much of northern and central Mississippi is flat, open farmland. WorldAtlas notes that this kind of landscape makes long-lived tornadoes likely.

A long-lived tornado stays on the ground for longer. That gives a single storm more distance over which to cause damage before it dissipates.

The state's settlement pattern shapes who takes the damage. Mississippi has few major metropolitan areas. Tornado impacts therefore often land on small towns rather than big cities.

Larger communities have not been spared. Jackson, the state capital, has had significant tornado events. So have Tupelo and Corinth, two cities in northeastern Mississippi. Both sit in the northern part of the state, where the most severe storms tend to occur.

WorldAtlas singles out a 1971 EF4 tornado around the Issaquena-Grenada area. That storm killed 58 people and injured almost 800.

An EF4 rating sits second from the top of the Enhanced Fujita scale. Only EF5 tornadoes rank higher.

The yearly average of 64 counts how often tornadoes touch down in Mississippi. It does not measure how strong they are. The 1971 storm shows what a single high-end tornado can do in a state where most storms fall on small communities.

Alabama averaged 63 tornadoes a year from 2003 to 2022, placing fifth in the WorldAtlas ranking of Storm Prediction Center data. Its 2011 tornado outbreak hit Tuscaloosa, northern Birmingham and two small towns farther north.

That count sits one tornado a year behind Mississippi, at 64, and three behind Oklahoma, at 66. Illinois ranks sixth with 57, six fewer than Alabama. Alabama's yearly total is a little more than half the 124 that Texas averages.

Those gaps are narrow. Alabama and Mississippi, two Southeast states, each sit within three tornadoes a year of Oklahoma, a Plains state.

WorldAtlas ties much of the state's tornado activity to its landscape. It reports that the Tennessee Valley across northern Alabama and the foothills of the Appalachian Mountains tend to funnel storms.

Those storms have reached the state's cities. Birmingham and Huntsville have both taken significant tornado hits.

In 2011, an EF4 tornado struck Tuscaloosa and the northern part of Birmingham. EF4 is the second-highest rating on the Enhanced Fujita scale.

The same year, an EF5 tornado hit the towns of Phil Campbell and Hackleburg. EF5 is the scale's top category, covering the strongest winds.

The two storms show the outbreak's range. The EF4 cut through urban areas, while the EF5 struck small communities. Both city and countryside took direct hits from the two strongest tornado ratings.

WorldAtlas estimates the 2011 damage at about $4.3 billion. It puts the death toll at about 240 people in Alabama, with about 110 more in nearby states.

Those estimates add up to roughly 350 deaths, more than two-thirds of them in Alabama. The toll beyond its borders shows the outbreak was a regional event, not a single-state one.

The 2003 to 2022 window behind Alabama's average includes the 2011 outbreak year. Over those 20 years, an average of 63 a year works out to roughly 1,260 tornadoes.

Illinois averaged 57 tornadoes a year from 2003 to 2022, the sixth-highest yearly count in a ranking of states built on Storm Prediction Center data. The state's history also includes the Tri-State Tornado of 1925.

That average puts Illinois six tornadoes a year behind Alabama, which averaged 63. It sits four a year ahead of Missouri and Iowa, which tie for seventh at 53. Illinois holds sixth place on its own, with no other state sharing its count.

Illinois lies where warm and cool air masses converge. That collision builds severe thunderstorms, which are most frequent in early summer. Most of the tornadoes these storms produce in Illinois are moderate in strength rather than violent.

The Illinois season starts well before summer. Roughly four of every five Illinois tornadoes form between the start of April and the end of June, according to the National Weather Service. They usually strike in the late afternoon or early evening.

Stronger tornadoes keep a similar calendar. The National Weather Service reports that April is the peak month for strong-to-violent tornado activity in its Chicago forecast area. May and June follow closely, and August rounds out the top four months. Those rankings come from one forecast office's territory around Chicago, not from statewide records.

Late-season storms are part of the record, too. Significant tornadoes in the eight-county Chicago metro area have occurred as often in November as in July, according to a separate National Weather Service analysis. For the Chicago region, the threat of a significant tornado does not end when summer does.

The Tri-State Tornado killed nearly 700 people, most of them in southern Illinois. No tornado in U.S. history has killed more. Its toll fell at the far end of the state from the Chicago area covered by the forecast-office figures. It stands apart in a state where most tornadoes stay moderate.

Missouri averaged 53 tornadoes a year from 2003 to 2022, tying Iowa for seventh place in the national ranking. That period includes the 2011 Joplin tornado, which earned EF5, the top rating on the Enhanced Fujita scale.

Missouri sits where the Great Plains meet the Mississippi Valley. That position puts it in the path of two contrasting air masses. Warm, humid air flows north from the Gulf. Cooler air arrives from the Rockies and the upper Midwest. Severe storms develop along the boundary where the two collide.

Tornado activity in Missouri peaks from April through June. The risk does not end with spring. Tornadoes can form in the state in any month of the year. A quiet stretch in late summer or winter does not rule out a storm.

In the ranking, Missouri trails sixth-place Illinois by four tornadoes a year. It sits seven a year ahead of Florida and Minnesota, which share ninth place. Texas, in first, averages more than twice Missouri's count.

Those numbers place Missouri in a tight middle band of the table. A handful of tornadoes a year separates it from the states just above and below it. The gap to the top is far wider. Missouri's tie with Iowa, its neighbor to the north, adds to that clustering.

Both of Missouri's largest metro areas have been affected. St. Louis sits on the state's eastern border along the Mississippi River. Kansas City sits on its western border. Each has been in the path of tornadoes. With major cities at both edges of the state, tornado risk in Missouri reaches well beyond farmland and small towns.

The Joplin tornado struck southwestern Missouri in 2011. It killed 158 people and injured well over 1,100 others. Its death toll is higher than those of Oklahoma's 1947 Woodward $WWD tornado and the 1953 Waco tornado in Texas. That toll came from a single tornado in a state that ranks seventh, not first, for how often tornadoes strike.

Iowa's tornado season peaks from May through July. The state averaged 53 tornadoes a year from 2003 to 2022, tying Missouri for seventh in the WorldAtlas ranking of Storm Prediction Center data.

Iowa's peak runs a month later than Missouri's, which stretches from April through June. It also carries past the broader May and June peak into midsummer.

WorldAtlas reports that wind shear and unstable air feed Iowa's storms during those months.

Wind shear is a change in wind speed or direction with height. It can set air spinning horizontally in the lower atmosphere. A thunderstorm's updraft can then tilt that spin upright.

Unstable air lets warm, moist air near the ground rise quickly. That rising air builds the tall storm clouds that can produce tornadoes.

Iowa has seen notable outbreaks in recent years, according to WorldAtlas. Some produced long-track tornadoes with paths of more than 25 miles. Others brought high-intensity storms. One outbreak in 2024 struck near Minden, Iowa.

Most of Iowa is rural, and much of its land is farmed. That landscape means tornadoes in the state more often damage fields than towns or cities.

Iowa's yearly count matches that of Missouri, its neighbor to the south. Illinois, across the Mississippi River to the east, averaged 57 a year, four more than Iowa.

Florida averaged 46 tornadoes a year from 2003 to 2022, based on Storm Prediction Center data compiled by WorldAtlas. That count ties Florida with Minnesota for ninth place in the ranking.

The tie links two states at opposite ends of the country. Florida is a southeastern peninsula. Minnesota sits on the Canadian border. Over the same period, both reached the same yearly average.

Missouri and Iowa share seventh place at 53 tornadoes a year. That leaves Florida seven a year behind them.

Florida tornadoes have caused severe damage. In one outbreak, the deadliest tornado passed through Kissimmee, a city in central Florida. The Morningside Acres Mobile Home Park and the Ponderosa Recreational Vehicle Park took the most severe damage from that storm.

Minnesota averages 46 tornadoes a year, tying Florida for ninth place in the WorldAtlas ranking built on Storm Prediction Center data for 2003 to 2022. It is the northernmost of the 10 states covered here.

The tie pairs Minnesota with Florida, the southernmost state on this list. The two share an identical yearly average from opposite ends of the country.

Minnesota's count sits well below the top of the ranking. Texas, in first place, averages 78 more tornadoes a year. Kansas, in second, averages 41 more.

Tornado season in Minnesota falls in June or July, according to NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory. The agency groups the state with the northern Plains and upper Midwest when it describes when tornado season arrives in different parts of the country.
