Monitoring Outgoing Webhooks: Failure Rates, Backlogs, and Alerts

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Posted on Nov 3 • Originally published at webhookadmin.com

When you send webhooks, two numbers tell you most of what you need: how long each endpoint has been failing, and how many deliveries are waiting to be retried. We cover what to measure, when to alert and whom, and how to warn people before disabling an endpoint.

When you send webhooks, two numbers tell you most of what you need: how long each endpoint has been failing, and how many deliveries are waiting to be retried. The first tells you a particular customer's receiver is broken. The second tells you your own sending pipeline is backing up. This post covers what to measure, when to alert and whom, and the order in which to warn people before you disable an endpoint.

Say you send 100,000 messages a day to 500 endpoints. One endpoint can be down all day, and if it receives 0.2% of the traffic, your overall failure rate moves by 0.2%. Meanwhile an endpoint that receives one message a day shows a 100% failure rate after a single blip. The overall rate is useful for trends; to spot a broken receiver, track how long each endpoint has been failing.

Add one column to your endpoints table: when the current run of failures started. Clear it on success, set it on failure if it's empty.

The same column drives both "alert after an hour" and "disable after five days."

The backlog is the number of deliveries that failed and are waiting for their next attempt. It jumps when many endpoints fail together, and also when your own sending breaks (DNS, egress network), so put a threshold on the environment-wide number separately from per-endpoint alerts.

Alert on stages, not attempts. Alerting on every failed attempt buries the alerts that matter.

Group alerts per endpoint, for example at most one an hour, so a long outage doesn't produce an alert per retry.

Most failing endpoints are broken on the receiving side: an outage, an expired certificate, a URL that changed. The customer is the one who can fix it. If only your team hears about it, someone on your team has to contact the customer every time.

Contentstack's single email on disable is one answer to alert noise. The trade-off is that without an earlier warning, the customer first hears about it after deliveries have already stopped.

Auto-disabling protects your workers from endpoints that will never answer, but from that moment the customer receives nothing. Give them a chance to fix it first:

Make the time to disable longer than your retry window. Disabling mid-window throws away events that could still have been delivered.

The project overview in Webhook Admin shows deliveries, success rate, failures and average duration over the last 24 hours, the retry backlog, the number of disabled endpoints, and daily successes and failures for the past 7 days. Alerts follow the stages above: an endpoint failing for an hour or a message out of retries (at most once an hour per endpoint), an endpoint auto-disabled after five days, more than 1,000 retries waiting in an environment, and usage thresholds. They go to Slack, Microsoft Teams, email, a signed webhook or Chatwork, with the endpoint URL and a dashboard link.

Rather than a threshold on the overall failure rate, alert per endpoint on duration, for example when an endpoint has been failing for an hour. One dead endpoint barely moves the global rate, while a single failure on a low-volume endpoint looks like 100%.

Yes. Most failing endpoints are broken on the receiver's side - an outage, an expired certificate, a changed URL - and only the customer can fix them. Stripe and Shopify both email the receiving developer when deliveries keep failing.

We get too many alerts. What should we change?

Group alerts for the same endpoint (at most one an hour, say) and alert on stages - failing for a while, retries exhausted, endpoint disabled - instead of on every failed attempt. Transient failures then stop drowning out real ones.
