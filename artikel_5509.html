Why your plane waits on the tarmac in winter: de-icing, explained

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Why planes wait on the tarmac in winter comes down to a clean wing: 10 parts of airplane de-icing, from glycol fluids to holdover time and remote pads

A winter delay at the de-icing pad looks like idle time from a passenger window, but it is a safety procedure running against a clock. The trucks, the colored spray and the slow line of jets all serve one goal. The aircraft has to be free of frost, ice and snow at the moment it begins its takeoff roll.

That requirement is the short answer to why planes wait on the tarmac in winter. A wing generates lift because air flows smoothly over its shaped surface. Contamination roughens that surface and changes how the air behaves. Aviation rules treat a contaminated wing as unfit for takeoff. Airlines build their cold-weather operations around that principle.

De-icing does two separate jobs. The first is removal: heated fluid sprayed under pressure clears what has already built up on the airframe. The second is prevention: a protective coating keeps new snow or freezing rain from sticking before the plane leaves the ground. Those jobs can happen in a single application or in two distinct steps.

Prevention is where the clock comes in. Protective fluids do not last indefinitely. Once they stop working, contamination can start to cling again. That limit shapes nearly every part of the process — where airports set up their de-icing operations, when crews request treatment and what happens if a departure slips.

The result is a sequence passengers see only in part. A plane may push back from the gate, taxi to a remote area, wait its turn at the de-icing trucks and then head directly for the runway. If a delay stretches too long after treatment, the aircraft may need to be inspected or sprayed again.

The 10 slides that follow are a selection of topics, not a complete guide to the subject. They cover the physics of why ice matters on a wing and the U.S. rule that defines a clean aircraft. They also explain the chemistry of glycol-based fluids and the differences between fluid types. Later slides walk through holdover time, one-step and two-step procedures and the logistics of de-icing pads. Others address post-treatment inspections and a form of frost that can appear even when the air is above freezing. The final slide looks back at two crashes, in 1982 and 1989, that are widely cited in discussions of why de-icing procedures tightened.

Frost, ice or snow on a wing breaks up the smooth airflow over its surface. That cuts the lift the wing can produce and adds drag. Even a thin, rough layer can change how the wing performs.

A wing makes lift by shaping the air that moves across it. The curved upper surface and the angle at which the wing meets the oncoming air turn that airflow downward. The wing is pushed up in return. The process depends on air staying close to the surface and following its contour from the front edge to the back.

Contamination interferes with that process. Ice and frost add roughness, and snow can build up into uneven lumps. A rough surface disturbs the thin layer of air moving right next to the wing, known as the boundary layer. Once that layer is disturbed, the airflow can separate from the upper surface earlier than it would on a clean wing.

Separated airflow produces less lift. The wing can stall — losing much of its lift abruptly — at a lower angle of attack than a clean wing would. The speed at which it stalls rises as well. A pilot flying the normal takeoff speeds for a clean wing may have a smaller safety margin than expected, or none.

Drag rises at the same time. Rough surfaces create more friction and turbulence. That makes the airplane harder to accelerate and slower to climb. Accumulated snow and ice also add weight.

Thickness is a poor guide to the risk. A layer of frost that looks harmless can have a texture rough enough to disturb the airflow. Contamination on the leading edge and the upper surface of the wing matters most. Those are the areas where the airflow is most sensitive.

The effects can also be uneven. If one wing carries more contamination than the other, the two sides may lose lift differently. That can make the airplane roll unexpectedly just after liftoff.

Takeoff is the phase where these effects carry the most risk. The aircraft is low, slow and pitched up for its initial climb, with little room to recover from a stall. Stall-warning systems are typically designed around a clean wing. A contaminated wing can stall before the warning activates.

U.S. airline rules forbid a takeoff when frost, ice or snow is adhering to a plane's critical surfaces. The aircraft must be free of that contamination when its takeoff roll begins, not only when it leaves the gate.

Aviation calls this principle the clean aircraft concept. For U.S. airlines, it is written into federal regulation at 14 CFR 121.629. That section sits in Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations, within Part 121, the set of operating rules for U.S. air carriers.

The rule names the surfaces that count: the wings, control surfaces, propellers and engine inlets. Its wording also extends to other critical surfaces. The named parts are examples, not a complete list. Control surfaces are the movable panels a crew uses to steer and balance the aircraft. They include the ailerons on the wings and the elevators and rudder on the tail.

Each of those parts does a separate job in getting a plane airborne. Wings generate lift. Control surfaces let the crew change the aircraft's attitude and direction. Propellers and engine inlets handle the airflow that produces thrust. The rule groups them together instead of singling out the wings.

The timing requirement is the part that shapes winter operations. A plane that is clean at the gate can collect fresh snow or frost while it taxis or waits in line for the runway. Under the clean aircraft concept, the gate condition does not settle the question. What matters is the state of the surfaces when the takeoff begins.

The regulation also requires airlines to plan ahead. A carrier operating when frost, ice or snow may reasonably be expected to adhere must have an approved ground de-icing and anti-icing program. That program sets out the procedures, training and responsibilities the airline uses to meet the clean aircraft standard.

The same section reaches beyond the ground. A flight may not be dispatched, continue en route or land if the pilot in command or the aircraft dispatcher believes icing conditions might adversely affect its safety. Together, these provisions turn a clean aircraft into a legal requirement for winter departures.

Aircraft de-icing fluid is a glycol-based liquid. It is made from either propylene glycol or ethylene glycol, blended with water and a set of additives. The glycol does the core work: it lowers the freezing point of the water on the aircraft's surfaces.

Both glycols are alcohols that mix completely with water. That property matters on a wing. When the fluid meets snow, frost or ice, the glycol blends into the melted water instead of sitting on top of it. The resulting mixture stays liquid at temperatures where plain water would freeze solid.

Ethylene glycol is the same compound used in most automotive antifreeze. Propylene glycol is its less toxic relative. It also appears in food, cosmetics and pharmaceuticals. Ethylene glycol is poisonous to people and animals if swallowed. That difference in toxicity is one reason many operators choose propylene glycol formulations.

The water in the blend is part of the design. Ground crews mix fluid to a strength matched to the weather and the outside air temperature. A diluted mixture can still hold down the freezing point enough for the conditions at hand.

Heat and glycol play different roles during removal. The fluid is warmed before it leaves the spray nozzle, and that heat melts contamination quickly. Spraying under pressure then knocks loosened snow and ice off the surface. The glycol keeps the leftover slush from refreezing in place.

Additives complete the formula. Surfactants, or wetting agents, help the fluid spread in an even film instead of beading up. Corrosion inhibitors protect the metal structure and components the fluid touches. Exact recipes are proprietary and vary by manufacturer.

The chemistry also explains why a treated surface can eventually refreeze. Falling snow or freezing rain keeps adding water to the film on the wing. Each addition dilutes the glycol, and the mixture's freezing point rises. Once enough water has mixed in, the film can turn to ice.

Glycol fluids for aircraft are separate from the chemicals spread on runways and taxiways. Airport pavement de-icers are typically based on compounds such as potassium acetate or sodium formate. Aircraft fluids are made to their own specifications for use on airframes.

Type I is the thin, unthickened de-icing fluid that ground crews spray hot to strip snow, ice and frost from an aircraft. It is usually dyed orange. It offers only brief protection against new ice forming afterward.

The orange spray is the most visible part of a winter departure. Passengers often see it sheeting down cabin windows and dripping off the back edge of the wing. That color is deliberate. A dye helps ground crews see where fluid has landed and where coverage is missing. It also sets Type I apart from the thickened fluids, which carry their own colors.

Heat does much of the work. Warm fluid melts frozen contamination where it lands. The force of the spray then pushes loosened snow and slush off the aircraft's skin.

Low viscosity explains both the fluid's strength and its limit. Type I contains no thickening agents. It flows easily into seams, hinges and the gaps around control surfaces. The same thinness means it drains off quickly. Once most of it has run away, little is left on the wing to absorb fresh precipitation.

That is why Type I is treated as a cleaning fluid first. It removes contamination well, but its window of protection is short. In steady snow or freezing drizzle, a coat of Type I on its own may not keep a wing clean through a long taxi to the runway.

Crews typically blend Type I with water before it goes on the aircraft. The mix is adjusted to the weather. Milder conditions call for a weaker blend, while colder ones need more glycol. The concentration has to stay within the limits set for that specific fluid.

Type I is built to a published industry standard. SAE International, the engineering standards body, covers it under Aerospace Material Specification AMS 1424. That document sets the requirements manufacturers must meet. As a result, airlines can expect fluid from different suppliers to behave consistently on the wing.

Type II and Type IV anti-icing fluids are thickened with polymers. They stay on an aircraft's surfaces far longer than thin de-icing fluid and stop new snow, sleet or freezing rain from bonding to a wing that has already been cleaned.

The thickening agents give these fluids a gel-like consistency at rest. Once sprayed onto a wing, the coat stays in place instead of running off the trailing edge within moments. That standing layer acts as a buffer. Falling precipitation lands in the fluid rather than on the bare skin of the wing.

The fluid absorbs that moisture and becomes more diluted as it does. While the mixture stays above its freezing point, contamination cannot stick to the surface underneath. Once enough water has soaked in, the layer stops protecting the wing.

Color helps ground crews tell the products apart. Type IV fluid is usually dyed green. Type II fluid is typically pale straw-colored. The tint also lets crews see whether a wing carries an even, complete coat or whether a patch has been missed.

A thick coat left on a wing during takeoff would disturb the airflow that produces lift. These fluids avoid that through a property called shear thinning. They are formulated to stay viscous while the aircraft sits still or taxis slowly.

During the takeoff run, the rush of air across the wing changes that. The fluid thins under the airflow, shears off and flows away as the aircraft accelerates. Most of the coat leaves the wing before the aircraft lifts off. That restores the clean airflow the wing depends on.

That design ties these fluids to aircraft that reach high speeds on the runway. A slow aircraft may not produce enough airflow to clear the coat before it lifts off. SAE standard AMS 1428 covers Type II and Type IV fluids. The same standard also covers Type III, a formulation intended for slower aircraft.

Thickened fluids can leave residue behind. Repeated applications can leave dried gel in gaps around flight control surfaces. Moisture can rehydrate that residue into a swollen gel, which can then freeze at altitude. Frozen residue in those gaps can restrict the movement of the controls. Maintenance programs for aircraft that are treated often with thickened fluids include checks of those areas.

Holdover time is the window during which de-icing or anti-icing fluid keeps frost, ice and snow from sticking to an aircraft. The clock starts when the final fluid application begins. It stops when the fluid fails.

That starting point matters. Protection is measured from the beginning of the last spray. It is not measured from the moment the truck finishes or the plane leaves the treatment area. Time spent completing the application, taxiing and waiting in line all comes out of the same window.

The fluid fails because of the precipitation it is designed to absorb. Glycol keeps water from freezing only while there is enough of it in the mix. Each snowflake or drizzle droplet that lands dilutes the fluid a little more. At some point the mixture can no longer stay liquid, and contamination starts to stick to the surface.

Four main variables decide how quickly that happens. The first is the fluid type. Type I, Type II and Type IV fluids each have separate holdover guidance. The second is concentration. A fluid mixed with more water has less glycol to spare and gives a shorter window than the same fluid applied undiluted.

The third variable is outside air temperature. The fourth is the kind of precipitation falling and how hard it is falling. Freezing rain, freezing drizzle, light snow and heavy snow each load water onto the wing at different rates. A heavier rate uses up the fluid's capacity faster.

Those factors interact, so no single number describes holdover time. Regulators instead publish holdover tables that cross-reference fluid type and concentration against temperature and weather conditions. In the U.S., the Federal Aviation Administration issues these guidelines, and Transport Canada publishes its own. Both are revised periodically as fluids and test data change.

Flight crews consult the table that matches the fluid applied and the conditions at the time. Many entries give a range rather than a single figure. The window is an estimate, not a guarantee. A shift in the weather after treatment can cut real protection below what the table shows.

One-step de-icing uses a single application of heated, diluted fluid to remove frost, ice or snow and leave behind temporary protection. Two-step de-icing splits those jobs. Crews clean the aircraft first and then apply a separate anti-icing fluid.

The split exists because removing contamination and keeping it off are two different tasks. A fluid that strips ice well is not necessarily the one that holds up best against fresh snow. Separating the steps lets ground crews match each fluid to its job.

In the one-step method, a single spray handles both. The heated spray loosens frost, ice and snow on the wings and tail and washes it away. The fluid left on the surface then resists refreezing for a limited period. The aircraft is sprayed only once, which keeps the procedure short.

In the two-step method, the first application only has to clean. Crews spray heated fluid until the critical surfaces are clear. They then follow with a second coat of anti-icing fluid, usually Type II or Type IV. That thickened coat holds up longer against falling snow than the cleaning fluid would.

The second coat has to go on before the cleaning fluid can refreeze. If the first layer froze, the anti-icing fluid would sit on top of fresh contamination. That would defeat the purpose of the treatment.

The protection clock also runs differently between the two methods. In a two-step treatment, the window is measured from the start of the second step, not the first. Time spent on the cleaning pass does not count toward the aircraft's protection. In a one-step treatment, the window starts with the single application itself.

Which method a crew uses depends on conditions at the airport. Light frost on a clear morning is a different problem from steady snowfall that keeps landing on the wings during taxi. U.S. airlines that may face ground icing conditions must follow an approved ground de-icing and anti-icing program. That program spells out the procedures their ground crews and pilots use.

Many airports carry out winter de-icing at remote pads or dedicated areas rather than at the gate. Aircraft line up there for the de-icing trucks, then taxi straight to the runway.

Those pads are often placed near the ends of departure runways. A plane that leaves the gate in snow may stop again within minutes. It can end up sitting behind other jets that are waiting for the same trucks.

The queue is driven by timing. Anti-icing fluid protects a wing only for a limited period, known as holdover time. A plane that is sprayed and then held too long can lose that protection before it reaches the runway.

For that reason, flight crews and air traffic controllers try to schedule treatment close to each departure. A jet treated too early may use up its protection while still waiting. A flight can therefore wait before it reaches the pad as well as once it is there.

On the pad, pilots and the de-icing crew coordinate by radio. The crew confirms when spraying starts and when it ends, and the pilots note the time. Many crews also shut off the air conditioning packs during spraying to keep glycol fumes out of the cabin.

After treatment, the aircraft taxis from the pad toward the runway instead of returning to the terminal. That one-way flow also keeps gates free for arriving flights.

The pads have a second job: capturing runoff. De-icing fluid is made from glycol, and much of it drips off the aircraft during spraying. A dedicated pad concentrates that runoff in one place instead of spreading it across gates and aprons.

Glycol that reaches streams and rivers uses up oxygen in the water as it breaks down. Low oxygen levels can harm fish and other aquatic life. Pads with drainage systems let airports collect the fluid before it reaches storm drains. Some airports send the collected fluid to treatment plants, and some recycle the glycol for reuse.

Not every airport works this way. Some treat aircraft at or near the gate instead. Wherever the spraying happens, the timing problem is the same. It is why a winter departure can involve a wait between pushback and takeoff.

After de-icing, the flight crew and the ground crew inspect the aircraft to confirm that its critical surfaces are clean. If the holdover time runs out before the plane starts its takeoff roll, it needs another inspection or a repeat treatment.

The ground crew's check comes first. Once the trucks finish spraying, the de-icing team looks over the wings, tail and other treated surfaces. The goal is to confirm that no frost, ice or snow is still stuck to them. The team then passes the result to the flight crew before the aircraft leaves the treatment area.

The pilots carry that responsibility into the taxi. As the plane moves toward the runway, they track the time left in the protection window. They also watch the weather. Heavier snow or a switch to freezing rain can make the protective fluid fail sooner.

Clear ice adds a complication. It can be hard to see on a wet, fluid-coated wing. For that reason, some inspections include a physical touch check of the wing surface rather than a visual look alone.

When the holdover window closes before departure, the plane cannot continue to the runway as planned. The surfaces must be checked again, or the aircraft must go back for another round of fluid. At a busy airport during a snowstorm, that can mean rejoining the line of planes waiting for treatment.

Frost does not need freezing air to form. Cold-soaked fuel frost appears when the fuel inside the wing tanks is very cold, even if the surrounding air is above freezing. It typically follows a long flight at high altitude, where the fuel chills to very low temperatures. If the plane then lands somewhere humid, moisture in the air can freeze onto the wing skin above the tanks.

As a result, a plane parked on a mild, damp morning may still need attention before its next flight. The frost sits on the wing itself. The crew has to deal with it under the aircraft's approved procedures before departure.

On-board wing heating systems do not replace this ground process. Those systems are designed to manage ice in flight. On the ground, the aircraft still has to begin its takeoff roll with clean surfaces.

Air Florida Flight 90 crashed after taking off in snow from Washington National Airport on January 13, 1982, and ice contamination was a factor. Air Ontario Flight 1363 went down at Dryden, Ontario, on March 10, 1989, after departing with contaminated wings.

The accidents involved different countries, different aircraft types and different circumstances. Each showed what happens when a jet leaves the ground with frozen deposits on surfaces that must be clean. Both are widely cited in explanations of why winter procedures tightened.

Flight 90 was a Boeing $BA 737-200 departing during a heavy snowstorm. Shortly after liftoff, it struck the 14th Street Bridge and fell into the ice-covered Potomac River. Rescue efforts in the river were broadcast live on television.

The National Transportation Safety Board investigated the crash. It cited the crew's decision to take off with snow or ice on the wing surfaces. It also cited the crew's failure to switch on engine anti-ice during ground operation and takeoff.

Without anti-ice, an engine sensor iced over. The cockpit gauges then showed more thrust than the engines were actually producing. The board also faulted the captain for not rejecting the takeoff after the first officer pointed out abnormal engine readings. The jet had been de-iced at the gate but then waited in a long departure line as snow kept falling.

Air Ontario Flight 1363 was a Fokker F28 jet on a scheduled flight that stopped at Dryden to refuel. Snow was falling during the stop. The aircraft took off without being de-iced, failed to climb properly and crashed into woods beyond the end of the runway.

Canada responded with a federal Commission of Inquiry led by Justice Virgil Moshansky. His final report, published in 1992, looked well beyond the cockpit. It examined the airline's operating practices, crew training and oversight by Transport Canada. It also produced a long list of safety recommendations.

The inquiry examined why the crew did not de-ice. The aircraft's auxiliary power unit was unserviceable, and Dryden had no ground equipment to restart the engines. The crew kept one engine running during the stop. Guidance at the time discouraged de-icing the F28 with an engine running.

The case later became a standard example in aviation human-factors training. The Moshansky report traced how scheduling pressure, equipment faults and company practices combined to shape the crew's decision to depart.
